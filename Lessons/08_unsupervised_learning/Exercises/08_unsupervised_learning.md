## What this exercise is for

Lesson 8's own account table had one honest dimensionality — three latent
factors behind eight observed columns — and one anomaly type: individually
ordinary values that broke the relationship between columns. Principal
component analysis (PCA) reconstruction error caught 37 of the 40 accounts
flagged; a plain two-dimensional (2-D) scatter of the same accounts on
their first two principal components would not have found them, because
the anomalies sat, on average, **closer** to the middle of that picture
than genuine accounts did (1.30 against 2.20), not farther — a "flag
whatever sits at the edge" reading of that same plot points in exactly the
wrong direction. It would be easy to leave the lecture believing that a 2-D
PCA plot is simply the *wrong* tool for spotting an anomaly, full stop, and
reconstruction error the *right* one, always.

That is not true either, and this exercise is built to show you when each
one is.

You get **two account books from the same fictional bank, Meridian**, built
the same way — the same two latent factors driving the same six features,
the same 3% of accounts overwritten with a fraud pattern. On one book, a
"look at the plot and flag whatever sits at the edge" detector does almost
as well as reconstruction error. On the other, that same detector catches
**not one single anomaly**, in ten independent draws of the data, while
reconstruction error keeps working. Neither ordering can be guessed from
the names of the two detectors; both can be predicted from twenty minutes
spent reading how each book's fraud pattern was built, which is what makes
this an unsupervised-learning exercise and not a coin flip.

That is the skill being assessed. Not fitting PCA — **reading how an
anomaly was constructed well enough to know, before you plot anything,
whether a picture will show it to you.**

**Submit** a single notebook, `exercise08_<surname>.ipynb`, that runs top to
bottom in the course container. Written answers go in markdown cells beside
the evidence for them.

## The two account books

`account_monitoring_data.py` ships with this exercise and needs no network.

```python
from account_monitoring_data import load_retail_accounts, load_business_accounts

retail = load_retail_accounts()      # personal current accounts
business = load_business_accounts()  # business current accounts
```

Each function returns a single DataFrame: the six features on file for
every account, plus a boolean `is_anomaly` column. **`is_anomaly` is for
scoring a detector after the fact, never for fitting one** — Meridian's own
fraud team does not have it, and neither should any PCA you fit below.

Six figures are on file for every account, retail or business:
`monthly_inflow_eur`, `monthly_outflow_eur`, `transaction_count`,
`avg_transaction_size_eur`, `night_transaction_ratio` and
`new_payee_count`. Both books' *genuine* accounts are generated by the same
process: two latent factors, `cash_flow_scale` and `activity_level`, drive
all six observed columns, plus each column's own noise — so the honest
dimensionality of either book is 2, not 6, and neither factor is ever shown
to any method below.

**Retail — a credential-stuffing takeover.** An attacker has phished a
login. They cannot move large sums immediately, so what shows up in the
account's activity for the week under review is several new payees added
and probing at night, when a genuine retail customer rarely logs in.
Everything about how much money moves through the account — inflow,
outflow, average transaction size, overall transaction count — is left
completely alone, still drawn from the same genuine process as every
honest account. Only two of the six columns move, and they move a long way.

**Business — a mule account.** A shell company is opened purely to launder
funds. Whoever set it up did not have a real business's books to copy, so
they picked each of the six numbers independently, one at a time, from
roughly the range genuine business accounts occupy for that column. What
they could not fake, because they did not know it existed, is *how the six
numbers move together* in a genuine account — all six trace back to the
same two latent factors, so they are correlated in a fixed way a
one-column-at-a-time guess cannot reproduce.

The two books are the same size (1,600 accounts each) and the same
contamination rate (3%), so neither sample size nor base rate is doing the
work below — both are held fixed on purpose. What differs is **how the
anomaly was built**, and that, not the name of any method, is what should
drive your prediction in Part 1.

Read the module docstring in full before you do anything else — it
describes both fraud patterns honestly and at length, and it is not
padding. The `TRUE_*` constants below the loader functions record exactly
how each book was built. **Do not read past the loader functions until
Part 4.**

Both loaders take `n_samples`, `anomaly_fraction` and `random_state`; leave
`n_samples` and `anomaly_fraction` at their defaults throughout, and vary
only `random_state` where a part below asks you to.

## Part 1 — Predict, before you plot anything that could give it away (20 marks)

1. Load both books and report, for each: the number of accounts, the number
   of planted anomalies, and the anomaly rate.
2. For each book, plot the six features split by `is_anomaly` — a pairwise
   scatter of a few feature combinations, or one strip/box plot per
   feature, whichever makes the anomalies easiest to see if they are
   visible at all. **Do not fit PCA or any detector yet.**
3. Then, in writing, **predict**: on which book would flagging "the points
   sitting farthest from the middle of a 2-D PCA plot" correctly catch most
   of the anomalies, and on which would it fail? Give a reason per book, in
   terms of lesson 8:

   - In the plots from step 2, do the anomalies stand out clearly on any
     single feature, or only — if at all — in how several features relate
     to each other?
   - Read the docstring's description (not the `TRUE_*` numbers) of how
     each book's anomalies were built. Does that construction sound like it
     would leave a mark on one column, or spread itself thinly across all
     six with no single column exposing it?
   - If an anomaly's values are each individually unremarkable, would you
     expect its distance from the middle of a 2-D summary of *all six*
     columns to be unusually large, unusually small, or unremarkable?

**What is being marked:** the reasoning, not the ranking. A confident wrong
prediction that engages with all three questions above scores well; a
hedged answer that commits to nothing does not. **Write the prediction down
before you run anything** — the rest of the exercise is much less
instructive if you do not.

## Part 2 — Measure (25 marks)

4. Standardise each book's six features. Fit PCA on the **full, unlabelled**
   data (never pass `is_anomaly` to anything that fits) and report the
   scree table — explained variance ratio per component and its cumulative
   sum — for each book. State, with a reason tied to the table, how many
   components you would keep for each book.
5. Using the number of components you chose, build **two detectors** per
   book, both flagging the 3% of accounts that look most suspicious (3% is
   Meridian's own estimate of its contamination rate — an estimate, not a
   certainty, the same way the handout's section 6.1 flagged its own
   top 2%):
   - **"Look at the plot."** Project the standardised data onto its first 2
     principal components and flag the accounts farthest from the origin
     (PCA centres the data before projecting, so distance from the origin
     *is* distance from the data's own centroid).
   - **Reconstruction error.** Using your chosen number of components,
     reconstruct every account and flag the accounts with the largest
     squared reconstruction error, exactly as section 6.1 does for Aurora's
     table.
6. Report precision and recall for both detectors, on both books.
7. A single run of a synthetic generator tells you nothing about whether an
   effect is real or a lucky draw of 1,600 accounts — lesson 5's point,
   applied here because there is no $y$ to cross-validate against. Repeat
   steps 4–6 at 5 further seeds of each loader (vary `random_state` only)
   and report each detector's recall as a **mean and standard deviation**
   across all 6 runs, per book.
8. State plainly, book by book: do the two detectors agree?

**What is being marked:** that no detector or its fitted PCA ever sees
`is_anomaly` except to be scored afterwards; that the number of components
is chosen from the scree table and the choice is stated, not guessed; and
that recall is reported with a spread across seeds, not read off one run.

One warning, worth a sentence in Part 3: if your business-book "look at the
plot" recall comes out at **exactly 0.000** — precision 0.000 too, every
single flagged account a false alarm — that is not a bug in your code.
Check it against a second seed before you conclude anything, and then
explain it; do not assume you have miscounted and quietly move on.

## Part 3 — Explain the inversion (30 marks)

The ranking is not the same at the two books. Explain why, in six to ten
sentences per book.

Support each explanation with a **measurement**, not an assertion.
Suggestions, though you may find better ones:

- for a claim that a book's anomalies do or do not stand out on any single
  column: for each book, compute the fraction of anomalous accounts whose
  value falls outside the genuine accounts' 5th–95th percentile range, on
  **at least one** of the six columns;
- for a claim about where anomalies land in the 2-D projection: report the
  mean distance from the origin, anomalies against genuine accounts,
  per book — and say explicitly which direction the gap runs, since the
  direction is the entire point here, not just its size;
- for a claim about the reconstruction-error gap: report the mean
  reconstruction error, anomalies against genuine accounts, per book, at
  your chosen number of components;
- for a claim about *why* the number of kept components matters: rerun your
  reconstruction-error detector at 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5 kept components (both
  books, one fixed seed) and report recall at each. Aurora's account table
  in the handout never had to confront a book where *more* components made
  detection worse — if you find one here, say at which component count it
  happens and why, in terms of what that extra component is doing on that
  particular book.

**What is being marked:** this part carries the most marks in the exercise,
and the measurements are what separate an explanation from a plausible
story. Lesson 8's handout makes several claims of exactly this kind and
backs every one with a number computed a second way, or at a second seed —
do the same.
