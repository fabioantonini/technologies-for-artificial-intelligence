## 1. Why this lesson exists

Every classifier in this course so far drew a boundary whose *shape* was fixed
before training started: a straight line for logistic regression (lesson 4),
boxes with sides parallel to the axes for a tree (lesson 7), a curve of the
kind a chosen kernel allows for a support vector machine (lesson 6). Training
only chose *where* that shape went.

A neural network learns the shape itself. It is built from one very simple
part, the **unit**, repeated and arranged in layers. The layers between the input
and the output are called **hidden layers**, because nobody supplies or labels
their values. This lesson is about what one unit does, what a few of them do
together, and how they are trained. It leaves out what a first course
does not need: the matrix form of the training algorithm, and the many tricks
that make very large networks train. Those are in the advanced version of this
lesson, in `Advanced/09_neural_networks_advanced/`, for anyone curious; nothing
there is examined.

### 1.1 Three problems

Most of the lesson follows **Meridian Instruments**, a fictional maker of
optical sensors, whose data is generated by `Notebooks/instrument_data.py`
with every rule published, so a model's result can be compared with the truth.

1. **The acceptance test.** Every sensor is measured on two calibration axes
   and passes when both offsets are *jointly* small. 3,000 sensors.
2. **The two-channel drift.** A sensor drifts on two channels; it can be
   corrected when both drift the same way, not when they drift opposite ways.
   800 sensors.
3. **Handwritten digits.** 1,797 real images of eight by eight pixels, ten
   classes, shipped with scikit-learn.

The test rig that records each sensor's verdict is itself wrong 3% of the time.
A model that reproduced the true rule perfectly would still disagree with 3% of
the recorded verdicts, so on the sensors **no accuracy can exceed 0.97**, and
every score is read against 0.97, not against 1.

The number to carry out of this lesson is a comparison: **a hidden layer is
worth 39 points on the acceptance test and 2.8 on the digits.** Section 5.5
explains why the same idea is worth so much on one problem and so little on the
other, and why that difference is the real lesson.

## 2. One neuron is one line

### 2.1 The perceptron, and the unit you already have

The **perceptron** (Rosenblatt, 1958) is the ancestor: weights $w$, a bias $b$,
and the rule "output 1 if $w^\top x + b > 0$, otherwise 0". Replace that hard
step with the sigmoid of lesson 4 and you have exactly a logistic regression
unit:

$$\hat{y} = \sigma(w^\top x + b), \qquad \sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}$$

Nothing here is new. What is new is treating this as a *component*, a **unit**
or **neuron**, which a network repeats many times. The function applied to the
weighted sum, here the sigmoid, is the unit's **activation function**, and its
output is the unit's **activation**.

![](one_neuron.png)

*One unit drawn two ways, with $w_1 = 1$, $w_2 = 2$, $b = -1$. Left: the inputs
are weighted and added, with a constant 1 carrying the bias, and the sum $z$ is
squashed by the sigmoid; the small plot shows the sigmoid against the step the
1958 perceptron used, both switching at $z = 0$. Right: the same unit as a map
of the input plane. Look at the black line $x_1 + 2x_2 - 1 = 0$: on it
$z = 0$ and $\hat{y} = 0.5$, and the unit's verdict is which side of it a point
lies on.*

**Worked example.** With those weights, notebook 01 computes four points by
hand. The point $(1, 1)$ gives $z = 1 \cdot 1 + 2 \cdot 1 - 1 = 2$ and
$\hat{y} = \sigma(2) = 0.8808$: class 1. The point $(0, 0)$ gives $z = -1$ and
$\hat{y} = 0.2689$: class 0. The point $(1, 0)$ gives $z = 0$ and
$\hat{y} = 0.5000$ exactly: it lies on the line, and the unit is undecided.

So the set of points a unit is undecided about, $w^\top x + b = 0$, is a
straight line in two dimensions, a plane in three, a flat "hyperplane" in
general. **A neuron is a line.** Everything one unit can express is "which side
of this line are you on, and how far".

### 2.2 The acceptance test

Each sensor has a gain offset (in decibels) and a phase offset (in degrees).
Notebook 01 splits the 3,000 sensors into 2,250 for training and 750 for
testing, then standardises each axis with a scaler fitted on the training part.

![](acceptance_data.png)

*Left: the raw measurements. Right: the same points after each axis is divided
by its own production spread, with the rule that made the labels. Look at the
right panel: a sensor passes inside a circle, and the few wrong-coloured points
along the circle are the rig's 3% of mistaken verdicts.*

The circle is not a teaching choice. Both tolerances are exactly 1.25 times the
production spread of their axis (0.50 dB against 0.40, 3.75 degrees against
3.00), so after standardising, a sensor passes when it lies within 1.25 of the
centre: a circle of radius 1.25.

### 2.3 What one line is worth

| | accuracy |
|---|---|
| always predict "accepted", on the 750 test sensors | 0.5480 |
| logistic regression (one line), on the 750 test sensors | **0.5507** |
| the rig's ceiling | 0.9700 |

![](linear_boundary_fails.png)

*The fitted logistic regression on the test sensors, with the true circle
dashed. Look at where the line went: almost off the picture, so that nearly
everything is called "accepted".*

**The predictable mistake, and why it is reasonable.** A result of 0.5507,
barely above always saying "accepted", looks like a bug: an optimiser that gave
up. Usually that diagnosis would be right. Here it is wrong. The fitted
coefficients are $(0.0096, 0.0701)$ with intercept $0.1892$, almost exactly a
constant model, and that is the correct answer. The accept region is a disc
centred on the origin: for every accepted sensor there is, on average, a
matching one on the opposite side. Whatever a line gains by tilting one way, it
gives back on the other side, so the best it can do is barely tilt at all.
**No straight line can enclose a region.**

## 3. A hidden layer: several lines and a vote

### 3.1 The smallest problem that needs one

![](drift_clouds.png)

*800 sensors with two drifting channels, in four clouds. Look at the colours:
the two clouds where both channels drift the same way (correctable) sit on
opposite corners.*

The rule follows from the electronics: one global correction removes a drift
that is the same on both channels, but not one that is opposite. In logic this
is the exclusive-or (XOR) of the two signs. Logistic regression scores exactly
**0.5000**: a line is worth nothing at all.

### 3.2 A network written down by hand

Both correctable clouds have $a + b$ near $+2$ or $-2$; both uncorrectable ones
have $a + b$ near 0. So the rule is "$|a + b|$ is large", and that needs **two**
boundaries, $a + b > 1$ and $a + b < -1$, which one line cannot provide. Two
hidden units can.

Each hidden unit here uses the **rectified linear unit (ReLU)**,
$\mathrm{ReLU}(z) = \max(0, z)$: zero for negative $z$, $z$ itself otherwise.

![](two_unit_network.png)

*The hand-built network. Hidden unit 1 computes $\max(0, a + b - 1)$, hidden
unit 2 $\max(0, -a - b - 1)$; the output unit adds them, ten times each, minus
1, and applies the sigmoid. Follow one cloud through it in the table below.*

**Worked example: the four cloud centres.**

| centre $(a, b)$ | $h_1$ | $h_2$ | $10h_1 + 10h_2 - 1$ | $\hat{y}$ | class |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $(+1, +1)$ | 1 | 0 | 9 | 0.9999 | 1, correctable |
| $(-1, -1)$ | 0 | 1 | 9 | 0.9999 | 1, correctable |
| $(+1, -1)$ | 0 | 0 | $-1$ | 0.2689 | 0 |
| $(-1, +1)$ | 0 | 0 | $-1$ | 0.2689 | 0 |

On all 800 sensors, with nothing trained, this network scores **0.9938**.

![](xor_hidden_space.png)

*Left: the sensors as measured, which no line separates. Right: the same
sensors as the two hidden units see them. Look at where the clouds went: the
correctable ones to $(1, 0)$ and $(0, 1)$, the others together at the origin,
and now one line separates them.*

That picture is the central idea of the lesson. **The hidden layer did not
classify anything. It moved the data** into new coordinates where the output
unit's single line is enough. Learning a network means learning such
coordinates, and that is called **representation learning**.

### 3.3 Forward propagation

Computing a network's output from its input is the **forward pass**. For one
hidden layer of $H$ units, with the $m$ examples stacked as the rows of $X$:

$$Z^{[1]} = X W^{[1]} + b^{[1]}, \qquad A^{[1]} = g\big(Z^{[1]}\big), \qquad
Z^{[2]} = A^{[1]} W^{[2]} + b^{[2]}, \qquad \hat{y} = \sigma\big(Z^{[2]}\big)$$

The superscript in square brackets is the layer, $g$ is the hidden activation
(here ReLU), $Z$ holds weighted sums and $A$ activations.

![](forward_pass_map.png)

*A network with two inputs, four hidden units and one output, with every symbol
in its place. Weights live on the edges: $W^{[1]}$ is $2 \times 4$, one column
per hidden unit. Each hidden node holds two numbers, its weighted sum $z$ and
its activation $a = g(z)$. Look at the bottom row: stacking all $m$ examples as
rows turns those per-node numbers into the matrices $Z^{[1]}$, $A^{[1]}$ and
$Z^{[2]}$.*

Notebook 01 writes this pass for the hand-built network in two lines of NumPy:
`hidden = relu(D @ W1 + b1)` and `y_hat = sigmoid(hidden @ W2 + b2)`.

### 3.4 Letting training find the weights

Nobody writes weights by hand in practice. scikit-learn's `MLPClassifier`
(multilayer perceptron) trains a network with one hidden layer of a chosen
size. Training starts from small random weights, and the starting point, set by
`random_state`, matters: notebook 01 trains twenty times per size on the drift
problem, with all 800 sensors used for training and scoring.

| hidden units | runs reaching 0.99 | median accuracy |
|---|---|---|
| 2 | 6 of 20 | 0.7381 |
| 4 | 17 of 20 | 1.0000 |
| 8 | 20 of 20 | 1.0000 |

![](drift_boundaries.png)

*The first run at each size. Look at the left panel: with two units, training
walled off three of the four clouds and stopped there, at 0.74.*

**The predictable mistake.** It is natural to think that if two units *can*
solve a problem, training two units will. Section 3.2 shows two units can; the
table shows training finds that solution in only 6 runs of 20, while the rest
stop at three clouds of four. A wider layer than the problem strictly needs gives training more chances to
start near a good set of lines, which is why networks used in practice are
wider than their task requires.

Back to the acceptance test, scored on the 750 test sensors:

| hidden units | mean of 5 runs | worst | best |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0.6048 | 0.5480 | 0.6493 |
| 2 | 0.7392 | 0.7240 | 0.7693 |
| 3 | 0.8120 | 0.7227 | 0.9160 |
| 4 | 0.8821 | 0.7227 | 0.9507 |
| 8 | **0.9445** | 0.9387 | 0.9493 |
| 16 | 0.9549 | 0.9520 | 0.9600 |

![](boundaries_by_width.png)

*The first run at 1, 2, 4 and 8 hidden units, with the true circle dashed.
Look at the shape of the boundary: one unit gives one line, two give a strip,
four a four-sided fence, eight an eight-sided one close to the circle.*

![](hidden_lines.png)

*The eight hidden units of that network, each drawn as its own line from its
column of the first weight matrix (`coefs_[0]`), with the network's boundary in
black. Look at how the output unit's vote over which side of each line a point
lies on encloses a region that no single line could.*

With eight units the mean, **0.9445**, is within three points of the ceiling of
0.97. From one line, 0.5507, that is **39 points**.

## 4. How a network learns

### 4.1 The loop

Training repeats four steps, many times:

![](learning_loop.png)

*Training as a loop. Look at the arrow back to step 1: after the weights move,
every prediction, every error and every blame has to be computed again; only
the weights carry over from one round to the next.*

One word in the figure needs a definition. A weight's **blame** is how much of
the error it is responsible for: precisely, its gradient, the rate at which the
loss changes when that weight changes. Section 4.3 computes it for one unit.

Two more words name how the data is fed in. A **mini-batch** is a small random group
of training examples, 32 in notebook 02; each round of the loop uses one
mini-batch, which is much cheaper than using all the data and still points
roughly the right way on average. An **epoch** is one pass through all the
mini-batches, so through every training example once. With 1,077 training
digits in mini-batches of 32 there are 34 mini-batches per epoch, and 60 epochs
make 2,040 rounds of the loop.

### 4.2 The loss

The loss measures how wrong the predictions are, and it is the one lesson 4
used for logistic regression, the **cross-entropy** (log-loss). For one example
with true label $y \in \{0, 1\}$ and prediction $\hat{y}$:

$$L = -\big[y \log \hat{y} + (1 - y) \log (1 - \hat{y})\big]$$

It is small when $\hat{y}$ is close to $y$ and grows without limit as a
confident prediction turns out wrong. For ten classes it becomes
$-\log(\text{probability given to the true class})$.

### 4.3 One gradient step on one unit

Each weight moves against its **gradient**, the slope of the loss with respect
to that weight, scaled by the learning rate $\alpha$ (lesson 3):

$$w \leftarrow w - \alpha \frac{\partial L}{\partial w}$$

For one sigmoid unit with the cross-entropy loss, the chain rule gives a
strikingly simple result. Write $z = w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2 + b$ and
$\hat{y} = \sigma(z)$. Then

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial z} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial \hat{y}} \cdot \frac{\partial \hat{y}}{\partial z} = \left(-\frac{y}{\hat{y}} + \frac{1 - y}{1 - \hat{y}}\right)\hat{y}(1 - \hat{y}) = \hat{y} - y$$

using the sigmoid's own derivative $\sigma'(z) = \sigma(z)(1 - \sigma(z))$.
Since $\partial z / \partial w_j = x_j$ and $\partial z / \partial b = 1$:

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_j} = (\hat{y} - y)\, x_j, \qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial b} = \hat{y} - y$$

In words: **the blame for the output is the prediction minus the truth, and
each weight's blame is that times the input it carried.** It is lesson 4's
logistic regression gradient, for one example.

![](one_unit_step.png)

*One step, every number shown. Forward in blue, blame in rust. Look at the last
line: one step moves the prediction from 0.881 to 0.663, towards the true
label 0.*

**Worked example.** The unit of section 2.1, $w = (1, 2)$, $b = -1$, sees
$x = (1, 1)$ whose true label is $y = 0$. Forward: $z = 2$,
$\hat{y} = \sigma(2) = 0.881$, loss $L = -\log(1 - 0.881) = 2.127$. Blame:
$\hat{y} - y = 0.881$, and since both inputs are 1, every weight's blame is
0.881. A step with $\alpha = 0.5$ moves each by $0.5 \times 0.881 = 0.440$:
$w_1 \to 0.5596$, $w_2 \to 1.5596$, $b \to -1.4404$ (the figure rounds them to
three decimals). The new $z$ is $0.5596 + 1.5596 - 1.4404 = 0.679$ and the new
prediction $\sigma(0.679) = 0.663$: closer to 0, with loss down to 1.089.

### 4.4 Backpropagation: the blame flows backwards

In a network, a hidden unit's weights do not touch the loss directly; they
affect it through the output. **Backpropagation** applies the same chain rule
one layer at a time, from the output back to the input: the output's error is
shared out among the hidden units in proportion to the weights connecting them,
each hidden unit scales its share by the slope of its own activation, and passes
it further back the same way.

![](blame_backwards.png)

*The backward sweep. Look at the direction of the arrows: the error starts at
the output and travels back along the same connections the forward pass used,
so one sweep gives every weight its blame.*

Two facts make it practical. One backward sweep costs about as much arithmetic
as one forward pass, so a gradient for every weight costs roughly one more
prediction. And the libraries do it for you: `MLPClassifier` and Keras both run
backpropagation inside `fit`.

### 4.5 The learning rate

![](learning_rate_regimes.png)

*The same network, two hidden layers of 64 trained with plain stochastic
gradient descent (SGD) on the digits for 60 epochs, at three learning rates.
Look at the right panel: at $\alpha = 0.001$ accuracy is still climbing when
training stops, at 0.5 it settles high, at 2.0 it never leaves 0.1.*

| learning rate $\alpha$ | final training loss | validation accuracy |
|---|---|---|
| 0.001 | 2.0456 | 0.4806 |
| 0.5 | 0.0007 | **0.9722** |
| 2.0 | 2.3195 | 0.1000 |

Too large, and every step overshoots, so the loss never comes down: 0.1000 is
what guessing scores on ten classes. Too small, and the network is still
learning when the epochs run out.

**The predictable mistake.** A rate too small gives low accuracy on both the
training and the validation data, which is exactly what a network *too small*
looks like, and the natural response is to make the network bigger. That
reasoning is sound in general: low training accuracy is the usual sign of a
model too simple. Here it is the wrong fix. **Try learning rates a factor of
ten apart before changing the network.**

## 5. A network in Keras, on the digits

### 5.1 The digits

![](digit_examples.png)

*Two training examples of each digit, eight by eight pixels. Look at how coarse
they are: some are hard for a person to read.*

The 1,797 images are split three ways, as lesson 5 asks: 1,077 to train, 360 to
validate (to compare designs), 360 to test (touched once, at the end). Each
pixel is divided by 16 so it lies between 0 and 1.

### 5.2 A network in three lines

```python
model = keras.Sequential([
    keras.Input(shape=(64,)),                  # 64 pixels in
    layers.Dense(32, activation="relu"),       # hidden layer: 32 units
    layers.Dense(10, activation="softmax"),    # output: 10 probabilities
])
model.compile(optimizer=keras.optimizers.Adam(1e-3),
              loss="sparse_categorical_crossentropy", metrics=["accuracy"])
history = model.fit(X_train, y_train, validation_data=(X_val, y_val),
                    epochs=60, batch_size=32)
```

`Dense` is a layer in which every unit sees every input. `compile` chooses the
loss, cross-entropy for integer class labels, and the optimiser, **Adam**, a
refined form of gradient descent that adapts the step for each weight. `fit`
runs the loop of section 4.1.

**Worked example: counting the weights.** The hidden layer has one weight per
pixel and unit plus one bias per unit, $64 \times 32 + 32 = 2{,}080$. The output
layer has $32 \times 10 + 10 = 330$. Total **2,410**, the number Keras prints.

### 5.3 Softmax: ten scores become ten probabilities

The output layer computes ten numbers per image, one per digit. **Softmax** makes
them positive and makes them add up to 1:

$$\mathrm{softmax}(z)_k = \frac{e^{z_k}}{\sum_{j=1}^{10} e^{z_j}}$$

The prediction is the digit with the largest probability. With two classes,
softmax reduces to the sigmoid.

![](softmax_example.png)

*Two validation images and the ten probabilities the network gives each. Look
at the difference: the 6 above gets 0.991 on one digit; the 1 below, which the
network gets wrong, has no digit above a third.*

**The predictable mistake.** It is tempting to read the largest probability as
how likely the prediction is to be right. Softmax says how *sure* the network
is, not how *right*: notebook 02 also finds a 4 called a 7 with probability
0.920.

### 5.4 Training, watched

![](training_curves.png)

*Loss and accuracy after every epoch, on the training and validation images.
Look at the first ten epochs: almost all the learning happens there.*

After the first epoch the network scores 0.2667 on the validation images; after
60 it scores 0.9639, getting 13 of the 360 wrong, and 0.9926 on the images it
trained on.

![](softmax_confusion.png)

*Which digits are confused with which, on the validation images. Look off the
diagonal: the mistakes are few and scattered, a 4 taken for a 7, a 9 for a 5.*

### 5.5 39 points against 2.8

Remove the hidden layer and the network is softmax alone: one weighted sum of
the pixels per digit, which is multiclass logistic regression. Three runs each,
on the validation images:

| architecture | parameters | validation accuracy | sd over 3 runs |
|---|---|---|---|
| softmax alone, no hidden layer | 650 | 0.9324 | 0.0035 |
| one hidden layer of 32 | 2,410 | **0.9602** | 0.0052 |
| one hidden layer of 64 | 4,810 | 0.9611 | 0.0045 |

(The single run of section 5.4 scored 0.9639; this table averages three.)

![](hidden_layer_worth.png)

*What a hidden layer is worth on each problem. Look at the two gaps: 39.4
points on the sensors, 2.8 on the digits.*

Is 2.8 a property of the digits? Notebook 02 draws a different train/validation
split and three other starting points: the gap comes out at 4.7 points. The exact
figure moves with the split; its size does not. **A few points on the digits,
about 39 on the sensors**: the same idea is worth roughly ten times more on one
problem than on the other, and the reason is the shape of each problem. On the sensors the answer depends on
the two offsets **together**: a sensor passes when the pair is near the centre,
and no weighted sum of the two can say "near the centre". On the digits each
pixel pushes the answer more or less **on its own**: ink here suggests a 0, ink
there a 1, and a weighted sum of pixels already gets most of the way.

**The predictable mistake.** Having seen a hidden layer take the sensors from
0.55 to 0.94, it is natural to add one by reflex. The instinct is sound when
inputs act in combination; on data where they act independently it buys little
and costs parameters, here almost four times as many (2,410 against 650) for
2.8 points. **Fit the linear
model first: the size of the gap tells you whether a network was needed.**

## 6. Training in practice

### 6.1 A network that memorises

Notebook 03 trains two hidden layers of 512 units on only 300 training digits:
301,066 weights and biases, a thousand per example.

![](overfitting_curves.png)

*200 epochs. Left: training accuracy reaches 1.000 within a few epochs and stays
there; validation accuracy stays roughly level. Right: look at the validation
loss: lowest at epoch 13, then climbing for the rest of training.*

The network has enough freedom to store the 300 answers outright, and it does.
The validation accuracy, 0.9472 after 200 epochs, hides the damage; the
validation *loss*, 1.4 times its lowest value by the end, shows it: the network
is growing more confident about the validation images it gets wrong.

### 6.2 Two remedies

**Early stopping** watches the validation loss and stops when it has not
improved for a set number of epochs (25 here), keeping the weights from the best
epoch. **Dropout** switches off a random 40% of the hidden units on every
training mini-batch, so no unit can rely on any particular other one. Three
runs each, scored on the test images, which nothing here was chosen against:

| method | test accuracy | sd over 3 runs | epochs run |
|---|---|---|---|
| 200 epochs, no remedy | 0.9426 | 0.0013 | 200 |
| early stopping | 0.9463 | 0.0035 | 39 |
| early stopping + dropout 0.4 | 0.9481 | 0.0035 | 50.3 |

Read the spread before the means, as lesson 5 taught. The best and worst rows
differ by 0.0055, under two standard deviations of the runs: enough to say the
remedies did not hurt, not enough to rank them. What early stopping certainly
bought is time, 39 epochs instead of 200.

### 6.3 Activations, and six layers that never learn

![](activation_functions.png)

*Left: the sigmoid and the ReLU. Right: their slopes. Look at the dotted line:
the sigmoid's slope is never above 1/4; the ReLU's is 1 wherever the unit is
active.*

Section 4.4's backward sweep multiplies the blame by the activation's slope at
every layer. With sigmoids that slope is at most 0.25, so after six sigmoid
layers the blame reaching the first layer is at most
$0.25^6 = 1/4096$ of what left the output: too small to move anything. This is
the **vanishing gradient**.

![](deep_training_curves.png)

*Six hidden layers of 32, identical but for the activation, plain SGD at
learning rate 0.1. Look at the sigmoid's line: it sits on 0.1, guessing on ten
classes, for all 80 epochs.*

The sigmoid network ends at **0.1000**; the same network with ReLU ends at
**0.9611**. This is why deep networks use the ReLU, and lesson 10 meets the
problem again in deeper networks.

### 6.4 Shuffle the pixels: nothing notices

Take one fixed reordering of the 64 pixels and apply it to every image.

![](permuted_pixels.png)

*Five digits as written, and the same five with their pixels shuffled. Look at
the bottom row: to a person they are noise.*

The one-hidden-layer network of section 5 scores 0.9602 on the digits as
written and 0.9630 on the shuffled ones, three runs each, a difference well
inside the run-to-run spread. A dense layer gives every pixel its own weight
and never uses which pixel is next to which, so a consistent shuffle changes
nothing it can see. For a photograph, where neighbouring pixels are the point,
that is a strange blindness: **permute the pixels consistently and nothing here
notices.** Lesson 10 is about networks that do notice.

## Summary

- **One neuron is one line.** One unit scores 0.5507 on the acceptance test,
  barely above always saying "accepted", and 0.5000 on the drift problem.
- **A hidden layer moves the data** into coordinates where a line is enough. Two
  ReLU units written by hand solve the drift problem, 0.9938; each hidden unit
  is a line, and the output unit votes over them.
- **Training finds such weights more reliably as the layer widens**: on the
  drift problem 6 runs of 20 succeeded with two units, 17 with four, all 20 with
  eight; eight units reached 0.9445 on the acceptance test, against a ceiling of
  0.97.
- **Learning is a loop**: predict a mini-batch, measure the loss, send the blame
  back with backpropagation, move each weight against its blame. For one unit
  the blame is $\hat{y} - y$ times the input.
- **The learning rate** has a narrow useful band: 0.001 still learning after 60
  epochs, 2.0 never settling.
- **39 points against 2.8**: a hidden layer is worth a great deal when inputs act
  in combination and little when they act independently (2.8 points on one split
  of the digits, 4.7 on another). Fit the linear model first.
- **In practice**: a large network memorises a small training set, which the
  validation loss shows first; six sigmoid layers never leave chance, the same
  with ReLU reaches 0.9611; shuffling the pixels changes nothing for a dense
  network.
