# What a network adds

- Every boundary so far had its **shape fixed in advance**
- Logistic regression: a line; a tree: boxes; a support vector machine: its
  kernel's curve
- Training only chose **where** that shape went
- A neural network learns **the shape itself**
- It is built from one simple part, the unit, repeated in layers: the
  layers between input and output are **hidden layers**

::: notes
This is the one-sentence thesis of the lesson, and it is worth saying slowly,
because students often arrive believing that what makes a network special is
its size. It is not. It is that the family of boundaries is not fixed before
training starts.

Put the question to the room: lesson 6's kernel machines also drew curved
boundaries, so what is actually different here? The answer is who chose the
family of curves. With a kernel you chose it in advance, and if you chose
badly no amount of data repairs it. With a hidden layer the data chooses.

Handout section 1.
:::

# The perceptron, and a unit you already have

- The **perceptron** (Rosenblatt, 1958): output 1 if the weighted sum is positive
- Swap that hard step for lesson 4's sigmoid
- The result is one logistic regression unit, `LogisticRegression`
- New today: the unit is a **component**, repeated
- Also called a **unit**, or a **neuron**

::: notes
Nothing on this slide is new mathematics, and saying so out loud is the point:
students should feel that they already own the building block, so that the
only genuinely new idea today is putting units side by side and one after
another.

The perceptron of 1958 used a hard step: output 1 or 0, nothing in between.
The sigmoid is the smooth version of that step, and it is what lesson 4 used.
So a logistic regression model is one neuron - lesson 4's slide 13 drew it as
exactly this unit, so they have seen the picture before.

Ask the room: what did lesson 4's logistic regression boundary look like on a
two-feature problem? A straight line. Hold on to that answer; the next five
slides depend on it. Handout section 2.1.
:::

# A neuron is a line

$$\hat{y} = \sigma(w^{\top}x + b), \qquad \sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}$$

::: notes
Read it out in words rather than in symbols: take a weighted sum of the inputs
with the weights w, add the bias b, and squash the result into the interval
from zero to one with the sigmoid. Call the weighted sum plus bias z.

The squashing does not change which side of the line a point is on, because
the sigmoid only ever increases. The output is exactly 0.5 where z is zero,
and the set of points where z is zero is a straight line in two dimensions, a
plane in three, a flat hyperplane in general. Everything one unit can express
is "which side of this line are you on, and how far". Handout section 2.1.
:::

# One unit, by hand

- Weights w = (1, 2), bias b = −1: the unit's line is x₁ + 2x₂ − 1 = 0
- Point (1, 1): z = 1 + 2 − 1 = 2, and ŷ = σ(2) = **0.8808**, class 1
- Point (0, 0): z = −1, and ŷ = **0.2689**, class 0
- Point (1, 0): z = 0, and ŷ = **0.5000**: on the line, **undecided**
- The unit's verdict is which side of its line a point lies on

::: notes
Do the first point on the board, slowly: one times one, plus two times one,
minus one, gives two; the sigmoid of two is 0.8808. Then let the room do the
second point themselves before you show it: z is minus one, and the sigmoid of
minus one is 0.2689.

The third point is the one to dwell on. It gives z exactly zero and an output
of exactly one half, because it lies on the line. Notebook 01 computes these
same points in five lines of NumPy, so the room will see these digits printed
again in twenty minutes.

Handout section 2.1, worked example.
:::

# Meridian's acceptance test

- Each sensor has a gain offset (decibels) and a phase offset (degrees)
- It passes when both offsets are **jointly** small
- 3,000 sensors: `train_test_split` keeps 2,250 to train and 750 to test
- `StandardScaler`, fitted on the 2,250 only, divides each axis by its
  spread (standard deviation)
- Both tolerances are 1.25 spreads, so a sensor passes inside a **circle of
  radius 1.25**

::: notes
The first real problem. Each sensor is measured on two calibration axes, and
the rule that decides whether it passes involves both offsets together, not
each one separately.

The split comes first and the scaler is fitted on the training part only:
lesson 1's and lesson 2's rules, unchanged.

The circle is not a teaching choice. Both tolerances are exactly 1.25 times
the production spread of their axis: 0.50 decibels against a spread of 0.40,
and 3.75 degrees against 3.00. So once each axis is divided by its own spread,
a sensor passes when it lies within 1.25 of the centre. Ask the room: can a
single straight line separate the inside of a circle from the outside?
Handout section 2.2.
:::

# No line encloses a region

![](linear_boundary_fails.png)

::: notes
The fitted logistic regression on the test sensors, with the true circle
dashed. Point at where the line went: almost off the picture, so that nearly
everything is called "accepted".

The fit did not fail. The coefficients are 0.0096 and 0.0701 with intercept
0.1892: almost exactly a constant model, and that is the correct answer for a
line. The accept region is a disc centred on the origin, so for every accepted
sensor there is, on average, a matching one on the opposite side. Whatever a
line gains by tilting one way, it gives back on the other side, so the best it
can do is barely tilt at all.

The sentence to leave in the room: no straight line can enclose a region.
Handout section 2.3.
:::

# The smallest problem that needs a hidden layer

- Correctable when the drifts a and b share a **sign**: the exclusive-or (XOR) of the two signs
- Logistic regression, all 800 sensors: exactly **0.5000**
- Correctable clouds have a + b near ±2; the others near 0
- The rule "|a + b| is large" needs **two boundaries**
- One line draws one boundary; two hidden units can draw two

::: notes
The exclusive-or is the traditional example, usually presented as an abstract
logic puzzle. Insist that this version is a physical situation: a drift that is
the same on both channels is removable by one correction, one that is opposite
is not, and the rule follows from the electronics rather than from a truth
table.

The last two bullets are the whole diagnosis. Everything depends on a single
number, the sum of the two drifts, and still no single line separates the
classes, because the question asked of that number needs two thresholds, not
one. Ask the room: how many straight lines would you need to draw by hand to
separate these four clouds? Two. Handout sections 3.1 and 3.2.
:::

# Two hidden units, drawn

![](two_unit_network.png)

::: notes
The network of the next slide, drawn before it is described in words. Two
inputs, the drifts a and b. Two hidden units, each a weighted sum followed by a
rectified linear unit, drawn as the small kink inside each node. One output
unit, the sigmoid of lesson 4.

Read the weights off the arrows: the first hidden unit adds a and b and
subtracts 1; the second subtracts both and subtracts 1. So the first wakes up
only when a plus b is above 1, the second only when it is below minus 1. The
output unit adds them with weight 10 each and bias minus 1, then applies the
sigmoid. Handout section 3.2.
:::

# Two units, written down rather than trained

- Each hidden unit applies an **activation** to its sum z: the rectified
  linear unit (ReLU), max(0, z)
- h₁ = max(0, a + b − 1) and h₂ = max(0, −a − b − 1)
- Output: ŷ = σ(10h₁ + 10h₂ − 1)
- ŷ at centre (+1, +1): 0.9999; at (+1, −1): 0.2689
- All 800 sensors, **nothing trained**: accuracy **0.9938**

::: notes
This is the moment the lesson turns. Do one cloud centre live on the board,
the plus-one plus-one corner: a plus b is 2, so the first hidden unit gives 2
minus 1, which is 1, and the second gives 0. The output's weighted sum is 10
minus 1, which is 9, and the sigmoid of 9 is 0.9999. Let them check the other
three centres themselves against the table in the handout.

The ReLU is used here as a tool: the maximum of zero and its input, nothing
more. Its properties come back after the break.

The sentence to leave in the room: whatever training does, this is the kind of
thing it is searching for. Handout section 3.2, worked example.
:::

# The hidden layer classified nothing

- The hidden layer **moved the data**
- Correctable clouds went to (1, 0) and (0, 1); the rest to the origin
- There, the output unit's **single line** is enough
- Learning such new coordinates from data is called **representation
  learning**
- Computing the output from the input, layer by layer: the **forward pass**

::: notes
This is the idea to carry out of the first half of the lesson, and it is worth
more than any algebra that follows. Students tend to picture a network with
several layers as a chain of classifiers, each one improving the previous
answer. That is not what happens. The hidden layer changes the coordinates in
which the data is described, and only the output unit classifies, with a
single straight line.

Ask the room: in the hand-built network, which unit made the decision? The
output unit, and it is the same kind of unit as logistic regression. The only
difference is what it was given to look at. Handout section 3.2.
:::

# Forward propagation, drawn

![](forward_pass_map.png)

::: notes
Computing a network's output from its input is the forward pass. This is a
network with two inputs, four hidden units and one output, with every symbol
in its place.

Weights live on the edges. The first weight matrix, W with superscript 1, is
2 by 4: one column per hidden unit. Each hidden node holds two numbers, its
weighted sum z and its activation a, which is the activation function g
applied to z. Point at the bottom row: stacking all m examples as rows turns
those per-node numbers into matrices, one row per example. With the 2,250
training sensors, m is 2,250.

Ask the room: how many numbers does the first weight matrix hold? Two inputs
times four units, eight, plus four biases. Handout section 3.3.
:::

# Forward propagation, written

$$A^{[1]} = g(XW^{[1]} + b^{[1]}),\ \ \hat{y} = \sigma(A^{[1]}W^{[2]} + b^{[2]})$$

::: notes
Name every symbol once. X is the matrix of inputs, one example per row. The
superscript in square brackets is the layer: layer 1 is hidden, layer 2 is the
output. W is a weight matrix and b a vector of biases. Z holds the weighted
sums, A the activations, and g is the hidden activation, here the ReLU. The
weighted sum inside g, XW plus b, is what the handout calls Z with superscript
1. The last step is one more weighted sum, put through the sigmoid.

Read it as two sentences: the hidden layer is a set of lines followed by a
bend of the ReLU at zero; the output is one more line, followed by a
probability. Notebook 01
writes this pass for the hand-built network in two lines of NumPy, relu of
D times W1 plus b1, then sigmoid of hidden times W2 plus b2. Handout section
3.3.
:::

# Letting training find the weights

- `MLPClassifier` (multilayer perceptron): one hidden layer, from random weights
- Drift, 800 sensors, 20 runs per size from different `random_state` values
- Runs reaching 0.99 accuracy: **6 of 20** with 2 units, 17 of 20 with 4,
  all 20 with 8
- In code: `MLPClassifier(hidden_layer_sizes=(4,))`

::: notes
Nobody writes weights by hand in practice. Training starts from small random
weights and improves them step by step; how it does that is the second half of
the lesson. Here the question is only whether it finds a good answer.

The predictable mistake: it is natural to think that if two units can solve a
problem, training two units will. The hand-built network showed that two units
can. Training finds that solution in only 6 runs of 20; the rest stop at three
clouds of four. The median run with two units scores 0.7381.

Ask the room why a wider layer would help when two units are already enough.
A wider layer gives training more chances to start near a good set of lines,
which is why networks used in practice are wider than their task strictly
requires. Handout section 3.4.
:::

# How a network learns: the loop

![](learning_loop.png)

::: notes
Training repeats four steps, many times. Predict: a mini-batch of examples
goes forward through the network. Measure: the loss says how wrong the
predictions are. Blame: backpropagation works out how much each weight
contributed to the error. Adjust: every weight moves a little against its
blame, scaled by the learning rate.

Point at the arrow back to step 1: after the weights move, every prediction,
every error and every blame has to be computed again. Only the weights carry
over from one round to the next.

Ask the room which of the four steps they have already seen in this course.
Steps 1, 2 and 4 are lesson 3's gradient descent; only step 3 is new.
Handout section 4.1.
:::

# Mini-batches and epochs

- A **mini-batch**: a small random group of training examples, 32 in notebook 2
- Each round of the loop uses one mini-batch
- An **epoch**: one pass through all the mini-batches
- Digits: 1,077 training images in batches of 32 make **34 per epoch**
- 60 epochs make **2,040 rounds** of the loop

::: notes
Two words that every network library uses, defined here because notebook 02
prints both. A mini-batch is a sample of the training data: each round of the
loop looks only at it, so one round is cheap, and the direction it suggests is
roughly right on average.

Do the arithmetic on the board: 1,077 divided by 32 is 33 and a bit, so 34
mini-batches, the last one smaller. Sixty epochs of 34 rounds each is 2,040
rounds.

Ask the room: if each round used all 1,077 images instead, what would change?
Each round would be about 34 times more expensive, and there would be only 60
of them. Handout section 4.1.
:::

# The loss and the gradient step

- Each weight moves against its **gradient**: how fast the loss (lesson 4's
  cross-entropy) changes as that weight changes
- w ← w − α · ∂L/∂w, with α the **learning rate** (lesson 3)
- A weight's gradient is its **blame**: its share of the error
- One sigmoid unit: blame **ŷ − y** at the output; each weight's
  blame is ŷ − y times its input

::: notes
Nothing on this slide is new either: cross-entropy is the loss lesson 4 used
for logistic regression, and the update rule is lesson 3's gradient descent,
with alpha the learning rate.

The one result worth stating is the last two bullets. For a sigmoid unit with
the cross-entropy loss, the chain rule collapses to something very simple: the
blame for the output is the prediction minus the truth, and each weight's
blame is that times the input it carried. The derivation is four lines in the
handout, section 4.3; do not do it on the board.

Ask the room: if the prediction equals the truth, how far does any weight
move? Not at all, because the blame is zero. Handout sections 4.2 and 4.3.
:::

# One step on one unit

![](one_unit_step.png)

# Reading the step

- Unit w = (1, 2), b = −1; input x = (1, 1), true label y = 0
- Forward: z = 2, ŷ = 0.881, loss **2.127**
- Blame ŷ − y = 0.881 for every weight, since both inputs are 1
- Step with α = 0.5: each weight down by 0.440, to 0.5596, 1.5596 and −1.4404
- New ŷ = **0.663**, loss down to **1.089**

::: notes
Go through it as arithmetic the room can check with a calculator. The loss for
a true label of 0 is minus the logarithm of one minus the prediction: minus
log of 0.119, which is 2.127.

The blame is 0.881 minus 0, and since both inputs are 1, each weight's blame
is the same 0.881. Half of that, because the learning rate is 0.5, is 0.440.
Every weight goes down by 0.440. The new weighted sum is 0.5596 plus 1.5596
minus 1.4404, which is 0.679, and the sigmoid of that is 0.663.

One step roughly halved the loss. Ask: what would two thousand such steps,
over many examples, do? That is what training is. Handout section 4.3, worked
example.
:::

# Backpropagation: blame flows back

![](blame_backwards.png)

::: notes
In a network, a hidden unit's weights do not touch the loss directly; they
affect it through the output. Point at the direction of the arrows: the error
starts at the output and travels back along the same connections the forward
pass used.

Each hidden unit receives its share of the output's error, in proportion to
the weight connecting it to the output. It scales that share by the slope of
its own activation, and passes it further back the same way. One backward
sweep gives every weight its blame.

Ask the room: a hidden unit connected to the output with a weight of zero, how
much blame does it receive? None. Handout section 4.4.
:::

# What backpropagation buys

- **Backpropagation**: the chain rule, applied from the output back
- Each hidden unit receives a share of the output's error, in proportion to
  its weight
- It multiplies that share by its activation's slope, then passes it back
- One backward sweep costs about one forward pass
- scikit-learn and Keras run it inside `fit`

::: notes
The name sounds technical; the idea is the chain rule of calculus, applied one
layer at a time, starting from the output.

The fourth bullet is why it matters. A gradient for every weight in the
network costs roughly one more prediction. Without that, training a network
with thousands of weights would be hopeless.

The last bullet sets expectations: in this course nobody writes
backpropagation by hand. The libraries do it inside fit. What the room needs
is the picture, because the picture explains the two failures in the last part
of the lesson, the learning rate and the vanishing gradient. Handout section
4.4.
:::

# Predictable mistake: a rate too small

- Two layers of 64, stochastic gradient descent (SGD: one step per
  mini-batch), 60 epochs, one run per rate
- Validation digits: 0.4806 at α = 0.001, **0.9722** at 0.5, 0.1000 at 2.0
- A rate too small looks like **a network too small**
- **Try rates a factor of ten apart** first
- In code: `keras.optimizers.SGD(0.5)`

::: notes
Name the mistake and say why it is reasonable. A rate too small gives low
accuracy on both the training and the validation data, which is exactly what a
network too small looks like, and the natural response is to make the network
bigger. That reasoning is sound in general: low training accuracy is the usual
sign of a model too simple. Here it is the wrong fix.

The final training losses tell the story: 2.0456 at the small rate, 0.0007 at
0.5, and 2.3195 at 2.0. The small rate has simply not finished learning when
the 60 epochs run out.

Ask the room: what is the cheapest experiment that tells the two diagnoses
apart? Change the learning rate by a factor of ten and train again. Handout
section 4.5.

# A network in Keras: 2,410 parameters

- **Keras**: the network library of lessons 9 and 10
- `keras.Sequential`: the layers in order, `keras.Input` then two `layers.Dense`
- A **dense** layer: every unit sees every input (32 ReLU, then 10 softmax)
- `compile` picks `Adam` (a refined gradient descent); `fit` trains
- Parameters: 64 × 32 + 32 = 2,080, plus 32 × 10 + 10 = 330

::: notes
Three lines inside Sequential describe the whole network: 64 inputs, one
hidden layer of 32 ReLU units, and an output layer of 10 units, one per digit,
with softmax. Dense is a layer in which every unit sees every input.

compile chooses the loss, cross-entropy for integer class labels, and the

2,410, the number Keras prints. Handout section 5.2.

# Softmax

$$\mathrm{softmax}(z)_k = \frac{e^{z_k}}{\sum_{j=1}^{10} e^{z_j}}$$

::: notes
Read it in words: the output layer computes ten numbers per image, one per
digit. Exponentiate each one, so they are all positive, then divide by their
total, so they add up to 1. The prediction is the digit with the largest
probability. They have met it once already: lesson 4's slide 58 used the same
softmax for three classes of drive.

With two classes, softmax reduces to the sigmoid: it is the same construction
at a different size. And the loss for ten classes is still cross-entropy,
minus the logarithm of the probability given to the true class.

Ask the room: if all ten scores are equal, what probability does each digit
get? One tenth. Handout section 5.3.
:::

# When to reach for a hidden layer

- Sensors: the two offsets act **together**
- Digits: each pixel pushes **on its own**
- The predictable mistake: adding a hidden layer every time, **by reflex**
- Digits: 2,410 parameters against 650, for 2.8 points
- **Fit the linear model first**: its gap to a network says whether a
  network is needed

::: notes
The reason is the shape of each problem. On the sensors the answer depends on
the two offsets together: a sensor passes when the pair is near the centre,
and no weighted sum of the two can say "near the centre". On the digits each
pixel pushes the answer more or less on its own: ink here suggests a 0, ink
there a 1, and a weighted sum of pixels already gets most of the way.

Name the mistake and why it is reasonable. Having seen a hidden layer take the
sensors from 0.55 to 0.94, it is natural to add one every time. The instinct
is sound when inputs act in combination; on data where they act independently
it buys little and costs parameters.

Ask the room: on a new dataset, what single experiment would tell you which
kind it is? Fit the linear model and a small network, and read the gap.
Handout section 5.5.
:::

# Reading the two curves

- One run: two layers of 512 on **300 digits**: 301,066 parameters
- Training accuracy **1.000**: the 300 answers are stored
- Validation loss lowest at **epoch 13**, 1.4 times that by epoch 200
- Validation accuracy stays near 0.95 (0.9472 at epoch 200), while the

::: notes
The network has enough freedom to store the 300 answers outright, and it does.
This is lesson 5's overfitting, seen inside one training run.

The point to make slowly is the difference between the two validation curves.
Accuracy only asks whether the top answer is right, so it barely moves. The

network is becoming more and more sure about the validation images it already
gets wrong. The loss shows the damage first.

Ask the room: which of the two curves would you watch to decide when to stop
training? The loss. That is the first remedy. Handout section 6.1.

# Two remedies

- **Early stopping**: stop after 25 epochs with no better validation loss; keep the best weights
- **Dropout**: switch off a random 40% of hidden units on each training mini-batch
- In code: `EarlyStopping(patience=25, restore_best_weights=True)`
- In code: `layers.Dropout(0.4)`

::: notes
Two standard remedies, both one line of Keras.

Early stopping watches the validation loss after every epoch. When it has not
improved for a set number of epochs, 25 here, training stops and the weights
from the best epoch are put back. It needs a validation set, which is lesson
5's discipline again.

Dropout is stranger. On every training mini-batch, a random 40% of the hidden
units are switched off, so the network cannot depend on any one unit and has
to spread what it learns. At prediction time every unit is on.

Ask the room to predict, before the next slide: which of the two will help
more on this network? Handout section 6.2.
:::

| method, two layers of 512 on 300 digits | test accuracy, mean of 3 runs | standard deviation, 3 runs | epochs run |
|---|---|---|---|
| 200 epochs, no remedy | 0.9426 | 0.0013 | 200 |
| early stopping | 0.9463 | 0.0035 | 39 |
| early stopping + dropout 0.4 | 0.9481 | 0.0035 | 50.3 |

::: notes
Three runs each, scored on the 360 test images, which nothing here was chosen

# The vanishing gradient

- Each layer multiplies the blame by its **slope**
- Slope at most 1/4 for the sigmoid; 1 for an active ReLU
- Six sigmoid layers: at most 0.25⁶ = 1/4096 of the blame reaches the first
- Six layers of 32, one run: sigmoid **0.1000**, ReLU **0.9611**
- This is the **vanishing gradient**

::: notes
The blame that reaches the first layer of a six-layer sigmoid network is at
most one four-thousandth of what left the output: too small to move anything.
The first layers keep their random starting weights, so the layers after
them receive inputs that carry nothing learned, and the network never leaves
chance.

This is why deep networks use the ReLU. The scores are validation accuracy on
the 360 validation digits after 80 epochs.

Lesson 10 builds deeper networks, and the problem comes back there. Ask the
room: knowing this, which activation would you choose for the hidden layers of
any network you build in exercise 9? Handout section 6.3.
:::

# What to take away

- **One neuron is one line**; a **hidden layer moves the data**
- **Learning is a loop**: predict, measure the loss, share out the blame,
  adjust the weights
- Low accuracy? First try learning rates ten times apart
- **Fit the linear model first**: its gap to a network says if one is needed

::: notes
Five sentences for the whole afternoon. If time allows, ask the room to say
each one back in their own words before you read it.

The first two are the first half of the lesson: a unit is a line, and a hidden
layer is several lines and a vote that changes the coordinates. The third is
the second half: training is a loop, and backpropagation is how the blame
reaches every weight. The fourth and fifth are the habits: diagnose the
learning rate before the network, and measure what a network is worth before
assuming it.
